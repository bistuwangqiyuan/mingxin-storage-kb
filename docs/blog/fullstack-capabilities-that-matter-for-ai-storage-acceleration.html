Full‑stack capabilities that matter for AI storage acceleration

Published 2026-09-10 · Xinyuanyi Technology Insights

AI training and inference workloads stress storage systems in ways classic benchmarks do not. For AI datacenter storage acceleration you need to evaluate an integrated set of capabilities — from media and fabric to kernel bypass, KV cache tiering, GPU enablement, and operational gate controls — and run joint tests with real model traffic. This article outlines the full‑stack capabilities that matter, how they affect measurable AI outcomes, and a repeatable evaluation checklist.

Why full‑stack matters for AI

AI workloads are sensitive to latency tails, throughput variability, and data locality. Improving raw SSD IOPS or capacity alone rarely moves the needle unless the storage path is optimized end‑to‑end. Key interactions include:

  • Storage protocol and fabric behavior under concurrent small reads (token sampling) vs large writes (checkpointing).
  • Host and GPU I/O stacks: DMA, GPUDirect/RDMA, kernel bypass, and driver maturity.
  • Cache placement: host NVMe/PMEM, KV cache tiers on storage nodes, and eviction policies tuned to model access patterns.
  • Observability: correlating token latency to I/O, fabric retransmits, and GPU stalls to diagnose bottlenecks.

A full‑stack view avoids optimizing one layer at the expense of another and enables reproducible gains on production models.

Core capabilities to evaluate

Below are the high‑impact capabilities and how to measure them.

  • NVMe‑oF (NVMe over Fabrics) with RDMA support

    • Why it matters: reduces host CPU overhead and network copy latency vs TCP, enabling near‑local NVMe performance across racks.
    • How to measure: 95th/99th percentile read latency under synthetic small random reads and under real inference traces; CPU usage per GPU.

  • KV cache tiering (storage‑side key‑value caches)

    • Why it matters: turns remote bulk storage into a fast cache tier for hot model parameters or embeddings; reduces network and backend SSD pressure.
    • How to measure: hit rate at production request mix, effect on tokens/sec, and end‑to‑first‑token (TTFT).

  • GPU enablement & joint optimization

    • Why it matters: features like GPUDirect RDMA, coordinated prefetch, and PCIe/NUMA topology awareness remove copying stages and prevent GPU stalls.
    • How to measure: time‑to‑first‑token (TTFT), GPU utilization variance, frequency of memory thrashing.

  • Kernel bypass and high‑performance drivers

    • Why it matters: minimizes syscall overhead and latency jitter; important for millisecond and sub‑millisecond response targets.
    • How to measure: microbenchmarks for round‑trip RPC latency, latency tail under load.

  • QoS, multi‑tenancy, and admission control

    • Why it matters: prevents noisy‑neighbor effects in multi‑model datacenters.
    • How to measure: SLO compliance under mixed workloads and controlled degradation behaviour.

  • Observability and telemetry

    • Why it matters: you can’t fix what you can’t measure. Per‑request tracing from app to SSD is essential for root cause analysis.
    • How to measure: time to detect/correlate a GPU stall to an I/O spike; availability of per‑token latencies.

  • Reproducible, signed benchmarks and gate‑based acceptance

    • Why it matters: vendor claims must be reproducible on your hardware and model mix. Gate‑based acceptance (joint test first, decisions second) with stop‑loss criteria prevents costly rollouts.
    • How to measure: ability to replay signed benchmark workloads; presence of third‑party attestation or signed reports.

Practical evaluation checklist (what to test)

  • Run joint storage+GPU tests with representative models (inference + TTFT; training checkpointing patterns).
  • Measure tail latency (p95, p99.9), TTFT, tokens/sec, and GPU utilization when storage is the suspected bottleneck.
  • Validate KV cache hit rates with production traces and confirm eviction policy behavior under bursty traffic.
  • Test failure modes: device loss, network path failures, and recovery time to SLOs.
  • Verify observability: per‑request logs that span app → host I/O → storage node → SSD.
  • Require signed, reproducible benchmarks and run a gate test in your environment before procurement decisions.

Key takeaways

  • Optimize the whole stack: media, fabric, drivers, cache policy, and GPU integration. No single layer alone solves AI I/O bottlenecks.
  • Focus on tail latency, TTFT, and GPU utilization as primary metrics; tokens/sec alone can hide latency problems.
  • Require reproducible, signed benchmarks and run joint gate tests with stop‑loss criteria in your environment before wide deployment.
  • Ensure observability and QoS controls are production‑grade to prevent noisy neighbor failures.
