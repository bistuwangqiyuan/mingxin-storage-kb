How to Verify Signed Inference Throughput Claims

Published 2026-09-05 · Mingxin Technology Insights

When a vendor publishes "signed benchmarks" that claim inference throughput gains or Time‑To‑First‑Token (TTFT) reductions, buyers need a reproducible, evidence‑based way to verify those claims before approving purchases. This guide gives a practical checklist and testing approach focused on real inference workloads and storage‑accelerated stacks (NVMe‑oF, KV cache tiering), plus specific checks you should expect from a signed report.

What a "signed benchmark" should contain

A credible signed benchmark usually includes:

  • A cryptographic signature or notarization linked to an entity (PGP/GPG signature, timestamping, or hash anchored on an external ledger).
  • Full test artifacts: config files, container images or hashes, driver/firmware versions, scripts, raw logs and CSV exports, and a clear description of the workload (model, batch size, token lengths, precision).
  • Environmental details: exact GPU models & firmware, CPU, NIC model and firmware, NVMe devices & firmware, kernel and driver versions, orchestration details (Kubernetes, SR‑IOV, RDMA setup), and power/thermal conditions.
  • Measurement methodology: warm‑up duration, steady‑state windows, sampling frequency, statistical treatment (mean, median, p95/p99), and how TTFT was measured.

If the vendor provides signed reports (for example, Mingxin Technology has published signed benchmark reports for their FX series all‑flash NVMe‑oF storage acceleration on a 480B model showing throughput and TTFT deltas), the artifacts above are what make the signature meaningful and verifiable. Reports should be downloadable and include checksums so you can verify integrity.

Step‑by‑step verification checklist

  1. Verify integrity and provenance
  • Check file checksums (sha256sum) and validate any PGP/PKI signature or timestamp. If the signature is opaque, request the public key or CA detail used to sign the report.
  • Confirm the signer: a vendor signature is not the same as an independent lab. Treat vendor‑signed reports as reproducible claims, not guarantees.
  1. Parse the test artifacts
  • Confirm exact model and tokenizer used (e.g., 480B family, sequence length, special tokens).
  • Note precision (fp16, bf16, int8), batch sizes, concurrency, and warm‑up policy. These change throughput and TTFT dramatically.
  1. Match environment parity
  • Recreate driver, CUDA, cuDNN, NCCL, OS kernel, BIOS, firmware and NVMe‑oF driver versions. Differences commonly explain single‑digit or larger percentage deviations.
  • Reconstruct network topology (RDMA/NVMe‑oF fabric, switch models, flow control); storage‑path latency is critical for KV cache tiers.
  1. Validate measurement methodology
  • Ensure warm‑up is adequate and steady‑state windows are long enough (minutes, not seconds) for realistic batching and cache behavior.
  • Check percentile reporting (p50/p95/p99) and whether reported throughput is peak or sustained. Vendors sometimes present peak numbers without clarifying sampling windows.
  1. Inspect raw metrics
  • Review GPU utilization, SM activity, memory bandwidth, and PCIe link statistics (errors, retrains).
  • For storage‑accelerated stacks, inspect NVMe latency distributions, IOPS, queue depth, fabric (RDMA) latencies, and cache hit/miss ratios for KV cache tiering.
  • Look for telemetry showing backpressure: CPU steal, interrupts, kernel scheduling delays, NIC drops.
  1. Reproduce at scale
  • Run the same workload on identical hardware or a close equivalent. Use the same data (or deterministic synthetic inputs) and measurement tooling.
  • Automate tests so you can rerun under different conditions and generate confidence intervals.
  1. Audit statistical confidence
  • Run multiple trials, compute mean and confidence intervals. Verify the vendor's reported improvement lies outside your experimental noise band.
  • Use hypothesis testing if you need to make a procurement decision based on a minimum expected uplift.
  1. Beware of workload engineering
  • Synthetic token streams, artificially constrained sequence lengths, or special batching can inflate throughput. Confirm the workload matches your production mix.

NVMe‑oF and KV cache specifics to verify

Storage acceleration claims often hinge on tail latency and cache behavior. Focus on:

  • Cache hit rate and eviction policy under realistic concurrency.
  • Latency distributions to cold objects and the impact on TTFT—TTFT improvements often follow from faster cold lookups or reduced fetchs to deep storage.
  • NVMe‑oF transport configuration: RDMA or TCP, flow control, MTU, and offload settings that influence end‑to‑end latency.

Practical tools and metrics

  • Signature and integrity: sha256sum, gpg --verify, openssl verify
  • GPU and system telemetry: nvidia‑smi, DCGM, Nsight Systems (nsys), perf, /proc and iostat
  • Network and NVMe: perfquery, ibstat/rdma‑cmd, nvme-cli, iostat, fio for microbenchmarks
  • Orchestration logs: kubelet logs, systemd, dmesg for driver issues

Gate‑based acceptance and stop‑loss

Adopt gate‑based procurement: require a signed report, then a narrow gate of in‑house reproducibility tests. If the delta is within an agreed tolerance, proceed to pilot; otherwise exercise a stop‑loss clause. This avoids buying solely on a vendor's peak numbers.

Key takeaways

  • Always verify signature, full artifacts, and environmental parity before trusting a throughput claim.
  • Ask for raw logs, container/image hashes, firmware and driver lists, and scripts to reproduce exactly.
  • Pay special attention to storage path metrics, cache hit rates, and tail latencies when claims cite NVMe‑oF or KV cache tiering.
  • Reproduce tests in your environment and run statistical analysis across multiple trials.
  • Use gate‑based acceptance with clear stop‑loss criteria to mitigate procurement risk.
